## Lede
A famous puzzle asks how long the coast of Britain is — and the honest answer is "it depends on your ruler". This plate asks the same question of a coastline grown by physics: the edge of a magnetic domain at its critical point.

## On the sheet
The whole portrait sheet is one snapshot of a magnet. Crimson lines trace the edge of one huge connected domain that wanders across the sheet, wraps around the borders and comes back in. Black outlines of every size — from blobs the size of a thumbnail to specks a few cells wide — float around it, each drawn with a line weight set by how much area it encloses. Thin dashed straight segments shadow the crimson edge, the coast as seen through a coarse ruler. Across the bottom, HOW LONG IS THE COAST is spaced out in big letters, with two lines of measurements beneath.

## The science
Measure a coastline with a 17 mm ruler and you skip every little inlet; shrink the ruler and new wiggles appear, so the length keeps growing. Here the same crimson edge measures 0.95 m with a 17 mm ruler, 1.40 m with 4.3 mm, and 2.39 m with 1.1 mm. That growth isn't a measuring mistake, it's a property of the shape: it has a fractal dimension, somewhere between a line (1) and a filled area (2).

The shape comes from the Ising model — a grid of tiny magnets that each want to agree with their neighbours — simulated exactly at its critical temperature on a 176 × 240 grid that wraps around like a torus. At that one temperature, domains appear at every size at once, and their edges become fractal. Theory says the true dimension of such an edge is exactly 11/8, about 1.38; three rulers on a finite sheet give 1.29, an honest measurement that undershoots. A second check lands much closer: a bond count of 0.1455 against Onsager's exact 0.1464.
